If you're importing from China in 2026, tariffs are not a footnote in your cost model — they're a major line item. For many product categories, the combined duty burden on Chinese goods is now 30-50% on top of FOB price. Ignoring this math until goods are at the border is how businesses get crushed by landed costs they didn't plan for.

This guide covers the major tariff programs affecting Chinese imports, how to calculate your real exposure, and strategies that are actually available to reduce your duty burden (and ones that aren't worth the risk).

## The Layers of China Tariffs

Tariffs on Chinese imports don't come from a single program — they stack. Understanding each layer is essential to knowing what you actually owe.

**Layer 1: Most Favored Nation (MFN) duties**

These are the baseline tariff rates that apply to all countries that have MFN trade status with the US (nearly everyone). Rates vary by product category from 0% to 20%+. You can look up MFN rates by HTS code using the US International Trade Commission's tariff schedule or [a free tool like lgistics.ai](https://lgistics.ai).

**Layer 2: Section 301 tariffs**

The Trump administration imposed Section 301 tariffs on Chinese goods in 2018-2019, organized into four "lists." The Biden administration largely maintained them. As of 2026, Section 301 rates range from 7.5% to 25% depending on the product, with List 3 and List 4A goods at 25% for most categories.

Notable high-tariff categories under Section 301:
- Industrial machinery and equipment: 25%
- Electronics components: 25%
- Consumer electronics (some subcategories): 7.5-25%
- Steel and aluminum products: varies (may also have Section 232 tariffs)
- Plastics and rubber goods: 7.5-25% depending on subcategory

**Layer 3: The "emergency" / reciprocal tariffs (in legal flux)**

This is the layer that has moved the most. In 2025 the administration used the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) to add roughly 10-20% duties on most Chinese goods. The **Supreme Court struck those IEEPA tariffs down on February 20, 2026**, ruling that IEEPA doesn't authorize tariffs. The administration replaced them with a **Section 122** global surcharge (10%, announced toward 15%) — which the Court of International Trade then struck down in May 2026, a decision now under appeal with duties still being collected while it proceeds. Translation: this layer is unsettled and changing, so don't hard-code it. [Check the current surcharge for your category](https://lgistics.ai) at entry, and see [what happened to the IEEPA tariffs](/blog/ieepa-tariffs-china-2025-2026) for the full story.

**Layer 4: Anti-dumping and countervailing duties (AD/CVD)**

Separate from the tariff programs above, anti-dumping duties apply to specific products where the US International Trade Commission has found that Chinese producers are selling below cost or receiving unfair subsidies. Common AD/CVD categories include solar panels, steel products, aluminum extrusions, tires, and certain chemical products. These can add 20-200%+ on top of all other duties and are product-specific.

## How to Calculate Your Real Landed Cost

Most importers calculate landed cost wrong because they add tariffs to FOB price. The correct calculation is more involved:

```
Landed Cost = (FOB + International Freight + Insurance)
            × (1 + Duty Rate)
            + Customs Brokerage
            + Domestic Freight
            + 3PL / Receiving
```

The duty rate applies to the **customs value** — which is typically the transaction value (what you paid the supplier), but can be adjusted by CBP in certain circumstances.

**Example:** A product with $3.00 FOB, $0.40 ocean freight per unit, and an illustrative combined tariff rate of 45% (MFN 5% + Section 301 25% + a ~15% emergency/Section 122 surcharge — the volatile layer above; verify current):

- Customs value: ~$3.40 (FOB + freight, simplified)
- Duties: $3.40 × 0.45 = $1.53
- Landed cost before domestic handling: $4.93

That $3.00 FOB unit just became a $4.93 unit before it hits your warehouse. If you were pricing based on $3.00, your margin math is broken.

## When Tariffs Make US Manufacturing Competitive

This is one of the most important outputs of the tariff calculation: at some tariff levels, domestic manufacturing becomes cost-competitive even though US labor costs are higher.

The crossover point depends on the product. For products with:
- High China tariff rates (30%+)
- Significant weight-to-value ratio (tariffs plus heavy ocean freight eat up the labor cost advantage)
- Low labor intensity (assembly-line processes where automation is available)
- High quality requirements (where rework and returns from overseas production erode savings)

...US manufacturing often wins on total landed cost. Our detailed [US vs China cost comparison](/blog/us-vs-china-manufacturing-cost-comparison) walks through the math for a specific product example.

Products where this crossover happens most often:
- Steel fabrications and machined metal parts
- Heavy plastic components
- Products requiring UL, NSF, or FDA certification with frequent audit requirements
- Items with volatile demand where shorter lead times matter more than unit cost

## Tariff Exemptions: What's Actually Available

**Exclusion processes:** The Section 301 program has had exclusion request processes, where importers can petition for a product-specific exemption, and a set of exclusions remains in effect. Historically, exclusions have been granted for products with no domestic manufacturing alternative. The current administration's approach to new exclusions is restrictive — don't build a business plan around getting one.

**First Sale valuation:** For goods sold through a middleman (trading company → US importer), customs allows you to declare the first sale value — what the trading company paid the factory — rather than what you paid the trading company. This requires documentation and customs broker expertise, but can reduce your dutiable value meaningfully if you're buying through intermediaries.

**Engineering redesign:** If your product can be modified to change its HTS classification (legitimately — not through tariff engineering schemes), a different classification might carry a lower rate. This requires a customs ruling request or attorney review and isn't something to do informally.

**Nearshoring:** Some importers are shifting production to Mexico, Vietnam, India, or other countries not subject to China-specific tariffs. These countries have their own manufacturing strengths and limitations, and lead times to establish a new supplier relationship are 6-18 months. It's a real strategy but not a quick fix.

## What Not to Do

A few "strategies" that are widely discussed but carry serious legal risk:

**Transshipment:** Routing Chinese goods through a third country (say, Vietnam) with minimal processing and labeling them as Vietnamese origin. CBP actively investigates this, and the penalties — seizure, fines, criminal liability in serious cases — are severe.

**Misclassification:** Deliberately filing goods under a lower-tariff HTS code than the correct one. Also federal customs fraud. The downside risk is not worth whatever you save in duties.

**Undervaluation:** Declaring a lower customs value than the actual transaction price. Same category of risk.

The tariff environment is complex enough that there are legitimate strategies worth pursuing with the help of a qualified customs attorney. The ones above aren't among them.

## Staying Current on Tariff Rates

Tariff rates on Chinese goods have changed multiple times in the past few years and are likely to continue changing. Before any sourcing decision, [look up your specific HTS code](https://lgistics.ai) to get current rates — don't rely on rates you found in an article from 18 months ago or a supplier who told you the tariff is "only 7.5%."

For regular importing, the cost of a customs attorney consultation ($300-500/hour) to review your HTS classifications and tariff exposure is easily worth it for any product category where duties exceed $10,000 annually.

## Bottom Line

China tariffs in 2026 are real, they're stacked, and they vary significantly by product category. The importers who do the math upfront — calculating full landed cost before falling in love with a $2.50 FOB price — are the ones who build sustainable businesses. The ones who treat tariffs as an afterthought discover they've built a product business on a margin that doesn't exist.

Know your HTS code. Know your combined tariff rate. Build both into your cost model before you commit to a supplier. And if the tariff math is pushing you toward US manufacturing, read our [US vs China cost comparison](/blog/us-vs-china-manufacturing-cost-comparison) to understand when that math actually pencils out.
