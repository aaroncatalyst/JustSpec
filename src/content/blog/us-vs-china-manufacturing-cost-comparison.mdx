"How does US manufacturing compare to China?" is one of the questions we get most often. People expect a simple answer — China is always cheaper, or tariffs have made the US competitive — but the real answer depends heavily on what you're making, in what quantities, and what you include in your cost model.

We'll use a specific, realistic product example to run the full comparison: a 500ml BPA-free plastic water bottle with a custom lid and silk-screen logo, ordered in quantities of 5,000 units.

## The Example Product and Assumptions

**Product:** 500ml custom HDPE water bottle, 2 custom colors, logo screen-printed on both sides, shrink-wrapped in sets of 6, master carton of 48 units.

**Quantity:** 5,000 units initial order, 10,000 units annual volume.

**Destination:** Distribution warehouse in Chicago, IL.

This is a real product category with real costs we can benchmark against actual quotes. The numbers below are representative — you'll see variation in practice — but the ratios are directionally accurate.

## The Full Cost Breakdown: US vs China

| Cost Component | US Manufacturing | China (FOB) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unit cost (ex-works) | $3.80 | $0.85 | Includes materials + labor + overhead |
| Tooling (mold) | $3,500 | $1,200 | Amortized to zero after ~3 runs |
| Tooling per unit (5k) | $0.70 | $0.24 | One-time cost spread over first order |
| Ocean freight per unit | — | $0.38 | LCL, Chicago port of entry |
| Domestic freight per unit | $0.18 | $0.22 | Drayage + last mile to warehouse |
| Import duty per unit | — | $1.02 | ~38% combined: MFN 6.5% + S301 25% + IEEPA 6.5% |
| Customs brokerage per unit | — | $0.04 | ~$200 per shipment ÷ 5k units |
| **Total landed cost** | **$4.68** | **$2.75** | First-order, including tooling |

At 5,000 units, China wins on steady-state cost by roughly $1.47/unit — about 37% cheaper. On a 5,000-unit order, that's a $7,350 difference per run.

That's meaningful. But it's not the whole picture.

## What the Unit Cost Table Doesn't Show

**Lead time and working capital**

A US manufacturer runs this order in 4-6 weeks from approved samples. A Chinese factory, given production + ocean freight, is 14-20 weeks. For 5,000 units that cost $12,500 in China-sourced inventory, that's 3-4 extra months of capital tied up and unavailable for other uses.

If you have to carry 3 months of safety stock to cover the lead time, your real inventory holding cost adds $0.25-0.40/unit annually. For fast-moving products or businesses with constrained working capital, this changes the calculus.

**Defect rates and quality costs**

We're not going to claim US manufacturing is universally higher quality — it's not. Plenty of excellent product comes out of China, and plenty of mediocre product is assembled domestically.

But when things go wrong, the cost of fixing overseas manufacturing problems is high. A failed production run from a Chinese factory means 14-20 weeks of delay while you resolve the issue and re-run. Rework in-country (if the factory will do it) or scrapping inventory and re-ordering costs are real. We've seen quality failures add $0.50-1.50/unit to effective landed costs for buyers who didn't do adequate pre-production validation.

For a new supplier relationship in China, budget for one sample iteration and one potential production issue. For a mature, tested relationship, quality risk is lower.

**Minimum order economics**

The 5,000-unit example works because both US and China factories can accommodate that quantity for this product type. Drop to 500 units and the dynamic shifts: many US shops will still quote it (with higher unit cost and higher tooling amortization), while Chinese factories may require a 2,000-unit minimum.

At 500 units, US manufacturing often makes more sense purely on tooling economics — you're not penalized as heavily for a small run.

## When US Manufacturing Wins

Based on what we see across hundreds of RFQs, US manufacturing tends to be more competitive — sometimes the clear winner — in these scenarios:

**Tariff-heavy categories**

Products in Section 301 List 3 or 4 categories face 25% tariffs, and IEEPA adds further exposure. For products with high tariff rates, the China cost advantage shrinks substantially. [Look up your product's current tariff rate](https://lgistics.ai) before assuming China will win on cost — for some categories, it won't.

**Heavy or bulky products**

Ocean freight is priced by weight and volume (whichever produces higher revenue). Heavy, dense products — steel parts, heavy rubber components, bulk packaging — accumulate significant freight costs. Combined with tariffs, US production can be price-competitive for products where ocean freight alone would cost $1+/unit.

**Products requiring domestic certifications**

If your product needs UL listing, NSF certification, FDA registration, or CPSC compliance, you're managing ongoing audits and relationship requirements. Running those from a domestic facility is dramatically simpler operationally.

**Speed-to-market situations**

When you need inventory replenished in 4 weeks rather than 16, US manufacturing is the only option. For seasonal businesses, trend-driven products, or companies that can't forecast demand 5 months out, domestic lead times have quantifiable value that doesn't show up in unit cost comparisons.

**Quality-critical applications**

For products where failures are expensive — liability exposure, high rework costs, customer-facing quality that drives returns — shorter supply chains with more quality touchpoints reduce risk in ways that are hard to price but genuinely matter.

## When China Manufacturing Wins

**Labor-intensive products**

Anything requiring extensive hand labor — complex assembly, detailed sewing, hand-finishing — is hard to compete with China on cost. US labor rates are 10-15× higher than Chinese factory labor rates, and that gap is significant for labor-intensive processes where automation hasn't caught up.

**Large quantities of light, compact products**

Small, lightweight products that ship dense (high units per carton) accumulate minimal freight cost per unit. Combined with China's labor cost advantage, the economics at scale are hard to beat.

**Categories with abundant Chinese specialty manufacturers**

Some product categories have deep, specialized manufacturing clusters in China that produce genuine quality at price points domestic manufacturers genuinely can't match — electronics components, certain apparel categories, standard hardware. For these, China isn't just cheaper; it's where the expertise lives.

**Electronics and PCBs**

US PCB fabrication and electronics assembly is possible, but for consumer electronics with high volume requirements, China's electronics manufacturing ecosystem has a depth and maturity that US manufacturing doesn't currently match.

## Running the Real Math for Your Product

The example above illustrates the framework, but your product will have different numbers. The inputs that matter most:

1. **Combined tariff rate** — [look this up by HTS code](https://lgistics.ai) before anything else. This is the most commonly miscalculated variable.
2. **Freight cost** — get an actual quote from a freight forwarder for your product's weight/volume profile. Don't estimate.
3. **Lead time premium** — how much is shorter lead time worth in your business? What does carrying 3 extra months of inventory actually cost you?
4. **Quality risk factor** — what's the expected cost of one production failure? For a $10,000 order, a 20% quality failure rate adds $2,000 in effective cost.
5. **Annual volume** — tooling amortization, freight rates, and supplier leverage all improve at scale.

## Bottom Line

The honest answer is: it depends. At 5,000 units of our example water bottle, China is about 37% cheaper on steady-state landed cost. At higher tariff rates, different product dimensions, smaller quantities, or with a different quality risk profile, that gap narrows or disappears.

What we consistently find is that the comparison is much closer than most people expect once they run the real numbers — including tariffs, freight, quality risk, and working capital. If you've been assuming China is always cheapest without running the full math, run it. You may be surprised.
