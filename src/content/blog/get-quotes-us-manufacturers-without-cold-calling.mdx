Here's the dirty secret of domestic sourcing: finding US manufacturers is the easy part. Directories like ThomasNet list tens of thousands of them. The hard part — the part nobody warns you about — is getting them to actually quote your job. It's common to email 40 or 50 US factories about a project and hear back from a handful, and get real quotes from fewer still. If you've had that experience and assumed you were doing something wrong, you probably weren't. The system is just built that way.

## Why US Manufacturers Ignore You

Understanding *why* the response rate is so low tells you how to beat it.

**They're busy with existing accounts.** A US contract manufacturer running near capacity has no incentive to chase a new, small, unproven customer. Quoting your job costs them engineering time they'd rather spend on the accounts already paying them. You are, from their side, speculative work.

**You look small and risky.** A cold inquiry from an unknown company, for a modest volume, reads as high-effort and low-reward. They can't tell if you're a serious buyer or someone who'll vanish after they spend two hours on a quote.

**Your inquiry is hard to quote.** Vague RFQs ("can you make this? what's the price?") without a real spec force the manufacturer to either chase you for details or pass. Most pass. An incomplete inquiry is easier to ignore than to answer.

**You contacted the wrong shops.** Many directory listings are for companies that don't actually do your process at your volume, or are trading/distribution outfits, not manufacturers. A chunk of your non-responses are simply mismatches.

## How to Actually Get Quotes Back

**Send a complete, quotable RFQ.** This is the highest-leverage fix. A manufacturer can quote a real [product spec](/blog/how-to-write-product-spec-manufacturing) — material, dimensions, tolerances, quantities, timeline — in minutes. Give them everything they need to say yes without a follow-up email. The easier you make it to quote, the more quotes you get. Attach a drawing or a reference sample if you have one.

**Signal that you're a serious, repeat buyer.** Manufacturers prioritize customers with a future. State your volume, your reorder cadence, and your timeline concretely: "Initial run of 5,000, expected quarterly reorders, PO ready this month." Specifics change how worthwhile you look, the same way they [move a factory's MOQ](/blog/moq-negotiation-lower-minimums).

**Qualify the shop before you send.** Don't blast 50 listings. Spend the time up front to confirm each one actually runs your process at your volume — check their capabilities, their typical customer size, their equipment. Twenty well-matched inquiries beat fifty scattershot ones, and your response rate on matched shops is dramatically higher.

**Make the first contact easy to answer.** A short, specific first message ("Do you run [process] for runs of [volume]? If so, I have a complete spec ready to send.") gets a yes/no faster than dumping everything in a cold email. Then send the full RFQ to the ones who bite.

**Follow up once, professionally.** A single polite follow-up a few days later meaningfully lifts response rates — inquiries get buried, not always rejected. One follow-up, not five.

## The Volume Problem

Even doing everything right, domestic quoting is a numbers game with a lot of drop-off: qualified shops → responses → actual quotes → competitive quotes. To end up with three or four comparable US quotes, you often need to start with a couple dozen well-matched, well-briefed inquiries. That's a real time cost, and it's the main reason importers default to overseas sourcing even when domestic might win on total cost — not because China is always cheaper, but because [Alibaba compresses the find-and-quote loop](/blog/thomasnet-vs-alibaba-find-manufacturers) into days while the US process takes weeks of chasing.

The entire reason quote-aggregation services exist is this response-rate problem. Instead of you personally emailing 50 shops and chasing quotes for weeks, the service maintains supplier relationships and sends your RFQ to matched, responsive manufacturers — so quotes come back because there's an existing relationship and a professional, complete brief on the other end.
