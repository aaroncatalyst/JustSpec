Reshoring — moving production back to the US from overseas — went from a talking point to a real consideration when tariffs on Chinese goods climbed and supply chains proved fragile. But "bring it home" is a slogan, not a strategy. For some products, domestic manufacturing genuinely pencils out once you do the full math. For others, it's an expensive reaction to headlines that erodes your margin for no real benefit. Here's how to tell which one you're looking at.

## Why Reshoring Is Even on the Table

Three forces moved the calculation:

**Tariffs.** Stacked duties on Chinese imports raised the effective cost of importing for many categories. When the combined tariff on a product is high enough, the gap between US and China unit cost narrows or closes. Note the caveat that matters: tariff rates have been volatile and legally contested in 2025–2026, so the exact number moves — [check current rates](/blog/china-tariffs-2026-importers-guide) before you lean on any of them.

**Supply chain risk.** The pandemic and the shipping chaos that followed taught importers that a 40-day ocean lead time and a single overseas source are a liability. Domestic production collapses lead times from weeks to days and removes a layer of geopolitical and logistics risk.

**Positioning.** "Made in USA" is a real marketing asset in some categories, commanding a price premium that can offset higher production cost.

All three are real. None of them automatically means *your* product should be made here.

## The Products Where Reshoring Actually Works

Domestic manufacturing tends to win — even against China's labor advantage — when the product has some combination of these traits:

**High weight-to-value ratio.** Heavy, low-value products get killed by ocean freight and the tariff, which is calculated on value but travels with all that weight. A heavy steel or plastic item that's cheap per unit often lands closer to US pricing than a light, high-value one.

**Low labor intensity.** If the product is made by automated processes — injection molding, CNC machining, stamping — rather than by hand, US labor cost is a small share of the total, so China's labor advantage barely applies. This is the single most important factor.

**High quality or compliance requirements.** Products needing tight tolerances, frequent audits, or certifications (medical, food-contact, safety-critical) carry hidden costs when made overseas: rework, returns, travel, and the risk of a bad batch you can't inspect in time. Domestic production shrinks that cost.

**Short lead time as a competitive edge.** If your demand is volatile or seasonal, being able to reorder in days instead of weeks is worth real money in avoided stockouts and lower safety-stock inventory.

**A "Made in USA" price premium** your customers actually pay.

Steel fabrications, machined metal parts, heavy plastic components, and certified products with volatile demand are the archetypes. If your product hits several of these, run the numbers seriously.

## The Products Where Reshoring Is a Mistake

The mirror image: reshoring usually loses when the product is **light, labor-intensive, and cost-driven** — apparel, small consumer goods assembled by hand, low-margin commodities. Here, China's labor advantage dominates, US capacity may not even exist at your volume, and the tariff, however high, doesn't close a gap that large. Chasing "Made in USA" on these products often means doubling your unit cost to serve a customer who won't pay the premium.

It's also a mistake when you reshore on emotion rather than math — reacting to a tariff headline without checking whether the current rate actually flips your specific product, or assuming US capacity exists when it doesn't. Some categories were offshored so completely that the domestic supply base is gone; you can't reshore to a factory that no longer exists.

## The Math That Decides It

Reshoring is a landed-cost comparison, not a unit-price comparison. Line up the two honestly:

- **China total:** FOB + ocean freight + current combined tariff + customs/brokerage + domestic freight + the carrying cost of a long lead time and larger safety stock.
- **US total:** higher unit price + minimal freight + no import tariff + short lead time + lower inventory carrying cost + any "Made in USA" premium you can capture.

When you include freight, the tariff, and the inventory cost of a 6-week pipeline — not just the sticker unit price — domestic gets more competitive than the naive comparison suggests. But it still loses on plenty of products. The only way to know is to price it both ways. ## If You Decide to Reshore

Two realities to plan for. First, US suppliers respond to inquiries at low rates — finding domestic capacity is easier than getting it to quote you, so budget time for [getting quotes from US manufacturers](/blog/get-quotes-us-manufacturers-without-cold-calling). Second, don't go all-in on the switch. Dual-source: keep your overseas supplier while you qualify a domestic one, so a failed reshore doesn't take down your supply. And [transition without disrupting your supply chain](/blog/switch-suppliers-without-disruption) rather than cutting over in one order.

## The Bottom Line

Reshoring is right when your product is heavy, automated, quality-sensitive, or lead-time-critical, and wrong when it's light, hand-made, and purely cost-driven. The tariff environment has moved the line for some categories, but rates change and the decision is always a full landed-cost comparison — never a slogan. Do the math on your specific product at the current rate before you move a single order.
