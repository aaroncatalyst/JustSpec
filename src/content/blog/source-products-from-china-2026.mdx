China manufacturing is still the most cost-effective option for most consumer goods, electronics components, and industrial parts — even with tariffs factored in. But the process is genuinely complex, and most first-timers underestimate how long it takes and how many things can go wrong between "I need this product" and "it's in my warehouse."

This guide walks through the entire process, start to finish. If you follow it, you can actually source a product. If you skip steps, you'll learn expensive lessons.

## Step 1: Write a Spec Before You Contact Anyone

The biggest mistake new importers make is contacting suppliers without a spec. You'll get back 40 wildly different quotes for 40 different products, none of which is what you wanted.

A good product spec includes:

- **Dimensions and tolerances** — exact measurements in millimeters, with acceptable ranges
- **Material specifications** — not just "plastic" but "food-grade PP, FDA-compliant, minimum 2mm wall thickness"
- **Color and finish** — Pantone codes where possible, surface finish (matte/gloss/textured)
- **Functional requirements** — load bearing capacity, IP rating, temperature range, certifications required
- **Packaging requirements** — retail box, inner carton, master carton dimensions and weight
- **Target quantity** — your initial order and projected annual volume (factories price differently based on scale)

If you're having product images or samples made first, that's fine — but you still need a written spec before RFQs go out. Suppliers quote to spec, not to vibes.

## Step 2: Find Factories (Not Trading Companies)

Most listings on Alibaba are trading companies — middlemen who don't manufacture anything. They source from the same factories you're trying to reach, add their margin, and act as intermediaries. Sometimes that's fine; often you're paying 15-30% extra for no added value.

**Finding real factories:**

- **Alibaba** — search for the product category, then filter by "Manufacturer" (not "Trading Company") in the supplier type dropdown. This isn't foolproof — many trading companies list as manufacturers — but it's a start.
- **1688.com** — Alibaba's domestic Chinese platform, priced in RMB, mostly actual factories. The barrier is that it's entirely in Chinese, requires a Chinese payment method to order samples, and requires a freight forwarder with a Chinese address. Worth it for serious buyers.
- **Canton Fair** — held twice yearly in Guangzhou, this is where serious buyers go. You can walk factory floors, meet production managers face to face, and see samples. The April/October timing means you plan ahead.
- **Trade references** — if you know other importers in adjacent categories, ask them who they use. A warm referral to a factory is worth 20 cold Alibaba inquiries.

**Verifying you're talking to a real factory:**

- Ask for their business license (营业执照) — it should show manufacturing as their business type
- Request a factory video call. Real factories will walk you through the floor on camera; trading companies will stall or show you stock footage.
- Ask which specific machines they have for your product. If they can't name the equipment, they don't make it.
- Check their Alibaba Trade Assurance order history — factories tend to have many repeat orders in the same product category.

Gold Supplier badges, verified status, and high star ratings are all pay-to-play or easily gamed. Don't use them as primary filters.

## Step 3: Send a Proper RFQ

Once you have 8-12 qualified suppliers (real factories or reputable traders with good references), send each a structured RFQ. Don't just paste your spec in a message — structure it clearly:

1. Product description and spec (attach the document)
2. Target quantity for initial order and projected annual volume
3. Sample request (typically 3-5 samples)
4. Required certifications (CE, FCC, FDA, UL — whatever applies)
5. Desired lead time
6. Incoterms preference (FOB is standard for most importers)
7. Deadline for quotes (give them 5-7 business days)

Expect to send this to 12 suppliers and hear back from 6-8. Of those, 3-4 will send useful quotes. The rest will send vague pricing ranges, ask clarifying questions, or ghost you entirely.

**Managing supplier communication:**

This is where it gets tedious. You're going to send 20-50 messages per supplier before you have what you need to make a decision. Common back-and-forths: material substitutions they propose, MOQ negotiation, lead time clarification, sample cost (refundable or not), payment terms.

Use a spreadsheet. Track every supplier, every quote, every conversation thread. It sounds obvious; most people don't do it and lose track by week three.

## Step 4: Evaluate Quotes and Order Samples

When quotes come in, don't just compare unit price. Build a true cost comparison:

- Unit price (FOB)
- Tooling/mold cost (amortized over your order quantity)
- Sample cost
- Shipping cost from port to your warehouse
- Import duties (more on this below)
- Payment terms and the cost of capital tied up in the order

**Samples:** Always order samples before placing a production order. Always. No exceptions. The sample should be made from the same materials, on the same production line, to the same spec as your production order. Confirm this explicitly — some factories make beautiful handmade samples and run garbage production.

Sample lead time is typically 2-4 weeks. Evaluate the sample against your spec, not against your memory of what you asked for. Check dimensions with calipers, test the function, stress-test the materials.

If the sample doesn't pass, give the factory one revision cycle. If the second sample fails, move on. Some factories are not capable of making what you need, and no amount of communication will fix that.

## Step 5: Understand Your Tariff Exposure

This is the step most new importers skip, and it's where costs blow up.

Every product imported to the US has an HTS (Harmonized Tariff Schedule) code, which determines the base duty rate. On top of that, products from China may be subject to Section 301 tariffs (ranging from 7.5% to 25%+ by category), IEEPA emergency tariffs (currently adding 10-20% in many categories), and anti-dumping duties on specific products.

The total duty burden on some Chinese goods is now 30-50% on top of FOB price. On a product with a 40% tariff, that $3.00 FOB unit becomes a $4.20 landed unit before shipping, handling, and your margin — often making the economics look completely different from what a raw Alibaba price suggests.

Before you finalize supplier selection, [look up your product's HTS code and current tariff rate](https://lgistics.ai). Calculate your full landed cost with duties included. This may change which supplier is actually cheapest, or whether China sourcing makes sense for your product at all compared to [US manufacturing](/blog/us-vs-china-manufacturing-cost-comparison).

## Step 6: Place the Production Order

Once samples are approved and your landed cost math works, you're ready for a production order. A few things to nail down:

**Payment terms:** Standard is 30% deposit on order, 70% before shipment (against copy of bill of lading). Some factories ask 50/50. Avoid paying 100% upfront. If you can negotiate 30/70 with the 70% paid after delivery (net 30), that's ideal but rare for new buyers.

**Production deposit:** Wire transfer (T/T) is the norm for most transactions. Alibaba Trade Assurance offers some buyer protection for orders placed through the platform. For large orders ($10k+), consider using a freight forwarder or trade finance company with escrow-like protections.

**Lead time:** Confirm the production lead time in writing, including what "lead time" means — does it start when your deposit clears, or when materials are ordered? A 45-day lead time that starts after a 2-week material procurement window is really a 59-day lead time.

**Pre-shipment inspection:** For any order over $3,000, hire a third-party inspector (QIMA, Bureau Veritas, or similar) to inspect goods before they're loaded. It costs $300-500 and has saved us from accepting failed production runs more than once. The factory won't love it; do it anyway.

## Step 7: Shipping and Import

Your freight forwarder handles this, but you need to understand what's happening:

- **FCL vs LCL:** Full container load vs less than container load. If your order doesn't fill a 20ft container (~25 CBM), you'll share space (LCL) and pay by cubic meter. FCL is more economical at scale.
- **Sea freight timeline:** 2-3 weeks from a Chinese port to a US West Coast port, 4-5 weeks to East Coast. Add transit time within China to port, customs clearance (typically 3-7 business days), and drayage to your warehouse.
- **Customs clearance:** Your freight forwarder or customs broker handles this. They need your commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, and possibly additional documentation for regulated products.

Total door-to-door timeline from "deposit paid" to "goods in warehouse" is typically 75-120 days for a new supplier relationship.

## The Real Timeline

Here's the honest timeline for your first China sourcing project:

| Phase | Duration |
|-------|----------|
| Spec writing and supplier research | 1-2 weeks |
| Supplier outreach and quote collection | 3-4 weeks |
| Sample evaluation and iteration | 4-8 weeks |
| Production | 4-8 weeks |
| Shipping (sea freight) | 3-5 weeks |
| Customs clearance and delivery | 1-2 weeks |
| **Total** | **16-29 weeks (4-7 months)** |

Plan accordingly. If you need product in October for the holiday season, you need to start in April at the latest.

## Bottom Line

China sourcing in 2026 is more complex than it was five years ago — tariffs are higher, supply chains have more complexity, and the verification work required to find real factories is non-trivial. But for most product categories, it's still the most cost-effective manufacturing option if you do it right.

The process works. It just takes longer than anyone thinks, requires more back-and-forth than anyone wants, and has more ways to go wrong than most guides admit. Go in with accurate expectations, do the verification work, order samples before committing to production, and calculate your real landed cost before you fall in love with any FOB price.
