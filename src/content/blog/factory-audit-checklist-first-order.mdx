The moment before your first deposit wire is the highest-leverage point in the entire sourcing process. Everything you verify now is cheap. Everything you discover after the money moves is expensive — and if the "factory" was a trading company hiding its markup, or an outright scam, it may be unrecoverable. A basic audit costs you an afternoon and, if you use an inspector, a few hundred dollars. Skipping it is how importers lose deposits and receive containers of the wrong product with no recourse.

You don't need to fly to Shenzhen. Most of what matters can be verified remotely, and the rest by a third-party inspector for a few hundred dollars. Here's what to check, in order of what catches the most problems.

## First: Is This a Factory or a Trading Company?

This is the question that determines everything else. A trading company is a middleman that resells factory output — it adds margin, adds a communication layer, and often obscures who's actually making your product and whether they're any good. Sometimes a good trading company is worth it: for small orders, complex multi-component sourcing, or categories where you need one point of contact. But you need to *know* which one you're dealing with, because the price, the lead time, and the accountability are completely different.

How to tell:

- **Ask what else they make.** A real factory makes a focused range of related products — all injection-molded packaging, all knit apparel, all sheet-metal enclosures. A "factory" whose catalog spans electronics, kitchenware, and cosmetics is a trading company. No single plant makes all of that.
- **Ask for the material and process by name.** A real factory answers instantly and specifically ("PP, injection-molded, we run 12 cavities"). A middleman gets vague, or says "let me check with production" for a question a manufacturer would answer in its sleep.
- **Request a live video walkthrough** of the production line making your product category, on WeChat or WhatsApp. Real factories do this in minutes. Evasion, stalling, or a suspiciously polished pre-recorded clip is your answer.
- **Cross-check the business license (营业执照).** Ask for it. It states the registered business scope — "manufacturing" vs. "trading/wholesale" — plus the registered capital and the exact legal entity name. This five-minute check is one most buyers skip.

None of this means never use a trading company. It means never use one *without knowing it*, and never pay factory-direct prices for middleman service.

## The Verification Checklist

Work through these before committing. Anything you can't verify is a risk you're choosing to accept — sometimes that's fine, but decide it on purpose.

```
BUSINESS LEGITIMACY
[ ] Business license obtained; scope reads "manufacturing"
[ ] Company name on license EXACTLY matches the bank account name (critical)
[ ] Registered capital and years in operation reasonable for the claim
[ ] Address verifiable (maps, third-party report, or inspector on site)

PRODUCTION CAPABILITY
[ ] Live video walkthrough of the line making your product type
[ ] They can name the material, process, and tooling for your item
[ ] Lead time is realistic (suspiciously fast = subcontracting or lying)
[ ] Stated capacity matches your reorder plans

QUALITY SYSTEMS
[ ] Relevant certifications provided AND verified at the issuer's database
[ ] Sample approved against your written spec before mass production
[ ] They accept a pre-shipment inspection without pushback
[ ] Defect / rework / return policy stated in writing

COMMERCIAL SAFETY
[ ] Payment to a COMPANY account, never a personal one
[ ] Terms stage the payment (e.g., 30% deposit, 70% before shipment)
[ ] Written agreement covering spec, timeline, and remedies
[ ] References or verifiable transaction history on the platform
```

## The Two Checks That Catch the Most Fraud

If you do nothing else, do these two. They catch the large majority of deposit-loss cases.

**Match the bank account name to the business license.** Payment to a personal account, or to a company name that doesn't match the license, is the single biggest red flag in overseas sourcing. Legitimate factories invoice from and receive payment into their registered company entity. A mismatch means one of two things: you're paying a middleman you didn't agree to, or you're being set up to lose a deposit with no recourse — a personal account is far harder to pursue than a registered company. The fix is simple and non-negotiable: refuse to wire until the beneficiary name on the bank details matches the company name on the license. A legitimate supplier will not blink at this; a scammer will suddenly have "an accounting reason" why the account is under a different name. That excuse is the tell.

**Verify certifications at the source, not the PDF.** A supplier can send you a beautiful FDA, ISO, or CE certificate that's expired, belongs to a different company, or is fabricated outright. Certificates carry an issuer and a number — check them against the certifying body's public database, or have your inspector confirm. An unverified certificate is worse than no certificate, because it gives you false confidence to skip the check that would have caught the problem. If your product needs [documented compliance](/blog/how-to-write-product-spec-manufacturing), treat the certificate as unverified until you've seen it in the issuer's records.

## What a Scam Actually Looks Like

The typical deposit scam isn't crude. The "factory" has a polished Alibaba storefront, responds quickly, offers a price a little better than everyone else, and pushes for a fast deposit to "lock in the production slot." When you ask for the business license, it arrives — but the bank account is a personal one, or the company name is slightly different, with a plausible-sounding explanation. If you wire, the communication slows, the ship date slips, and eventually the account goes quiet. The whole thing is designed to feel legitimate right up to the wire. The bank-name check and a refusal to be rushed defeat almost all of it.

## Use a Third-Party Inspection

For a first order of any real size, a pre-shipment inspection from an independent firm runs roughly $300–500 and is the best insurance in importing. The inspector visits the factory, confirms it exists and operates, checks your goods against your spec (dimensions, weight, finish, quantity, packaging), and reports *before* you release the final payment.

The structural move that makes this powerful: tie your **70% balance payment to a passed inspection**. Now your money is the incentive — the factory has every reason to get it right, because they don't get paid until an independent party confirms they did. On a $15,000 order, a $400 inspection is 2.7% of the order value to convert your final payment into leverage and catch a bad run before it's on the water. That math is not close.

This is exactly why a written [product spec](/blog/how-to-write-product-spec-manufacturing) matters so much: the inspection is only meaningful if there's a documented standard to inspect against. "It looks fine" is not an inspection result; "meets spec on all 11 checked dimensions, 2 minor cosmetic defects in 320-unit sample" is.

## The Bottom Line

Verification isn't about distrust — it's about making the relationship survivable. The factories worth working with expect these questions and answer them easily; the ones that resist are telling you what you need to know. Do the checks before the wire, insist the beneficiary matches the license, stage your payments, and keep the balance conditional on a passed inspection.
